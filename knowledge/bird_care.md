# Bird Care
This file covers routine daily, weekly, and yearly care for small pet parrots such as budgies (parakeets) and cockatiels. Not a substitute for advice from a veterinarian.

## Bird feeding
Formulated pellets should make up about 75-80% of the diet, with fruits, vegetables, and greens no more than 20-25%; seeds should be only a small part. Offer pellets first thing in the morning, when the bird is hungriest. Remove fresh produce after a couple of hours so it does not spoil. Fresh, clean water must always be available: change it at least once a day (twice is better) and wash food and water dishes daily with soap and water. Never feed avocado, onion, chocolate, caffeine, or alcohol, and remove apple seeds. For example: 07:30 fresh water and pellets, 08:00 chopped vegetables, 10:00 remove leftover produce, 18:00 change water again.

## Bird water and habitat cleaning
Every day, replace the paper lining the bottom of the cage and clean and refill the water bowl and bottle. Once a week, do a full cage clean: remove and wash the tray and perches, check toys for loose or broken parts, clean the whole cage with warm soapy water or a bird-safe disinfectant, then rinse well and dry everything before the bird goes back in. Keep the cage away from the kitchen and other fumes, because birds are very sensitive to fumes such as those from overheated non-stick (Teflon) cookware.

## Bird exercise and enrichment
Let your bird out of the cage every day; a tame budgie needs at least 1 hour of out-of-cage exercise daily in a safe room. Before letting it out, close windows and doors, cover windows and mirrors, and turn off fans and heaters. Provide safe chew toys and change them regularly; natural wood branches, paper, and cardboard are good to chew. A radio or TV left on low can help keep a bird from getting bored when alone. For example: out-of-cage time from 17:00 to 18:00.

## Bird grooming
Offer a bath as often as the bird likes it, using a shallow dish of water or a light mist from a spray bottle; change bath water daily. A healthy beak rarely needs trimming, and you should never trim a beak at home; an overgrown beak can signal illness and needs an avian vet. Natural branch perches help keep nails worn down. Nails can be trimmed by your vet during check-ups; if you trim at home, take a little at a time and keep styptic powder ready in case of bleeding. Tell your vet about any change in beak or nail shape, color, or growth.

## Bird health care and vet visits
Take a new bird to a veterinarian experienced with birds as soon as possible after you get it. After that, schedule a check-up with an avian vet at least once a year; VCA recommends every 6 months for budgies. Check-ups can include a physical exam, nail or wing trims, and blood or stool tests to catch hidden illness early. Between visits, watch your bird daily for fluffed-up feathers, sitting on the cage floor, changes in droppings, weight change, sneezing, or crusty eyes or beak, and call your vet if you see them.
